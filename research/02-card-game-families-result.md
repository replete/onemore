# A Structured Map of Card Games: Families, Mechanics, Building Blocks and Variants

> Result for [02 — Card game families and building blocks](02-card-game-families-prompt.md). Produced by an external deep-research tool; content is unedited except that inline citations have been reformatted as Markdown footnotes (see [Footnotes](#footnotes) at the end).

Card games are best classified by **what you do on your turn**, which is the mechanism-first scheme David Parlett introduced and pagat.com uses. Under it, almost every traditional and modern card game is assembled from a small, reusable kit: a deck, a dealing pattern, a set of zones, a rank or trump order, one core play structure (trick, meld, match or beat, capture, compare, bet), and a scoring and end rule. The families differ mainly in which play structure they put at the centre, and the well-known folk games (Shithead, Crazy Eights, Cheat, Pontoon, Snap) differ from one table to the next mainly in a few predictable switches: which ranks have special powers, when you may draw, what counts as a tie, and whether penalties stack.

## TL;DR

- **Classify by mechanism, not by objective or deck.** Pagat's five top-level categories are outplay, exchange, comparison, layout and other.[^1] Within them, the working families are: trick-taking; beating and climbing; matching and shedding (the Eights and Stops groups); draw-and-discard (rummy); asking and passing (quartet and commerce games); fishing and capture; adding; vying and showdown; banking; war and slap; patience. Modern proprietary designs add deck-building, drafting and constructed-deck combat. The classifications mainly disagree on edge cases: War, Cheat, Spit, Egyptian Ratscrew and Shithead each get filed under two or more different headings.
- **The recurring building blocks are few.** About eight primitives cover nearly all games: deck modification, deal pattern, zones, rank and trump order, play structure, information hiding, timing (turn-based, simultaneous or out-of-turn), and scoring and end condition. Most "variants" of famous folk games are changes to just two of these: the special-card table and the draw or penalty rules.
- **For casual groups of 2–8 with newcomers,** the best picks are Cheat, Crazy Eights or Switch, Spoons or Pig, Shithead, and home-style Pontoon. They take minutes to teach, rounds are short, everyone interacts on every turn, and a latecomer can join at the next deal. Whist-family and Bridge-style games reward prior knowledge and fixed seats, so they suit casual play poorly.

## Key Findings

1. **Pagat's scheme is the most usable backbone.** Pagat's classified index explains that "Following Parlett, the main classification used to organise games on this web site is by mechanism: games are categorised according to the process for playing them - i.e. what you do when it is your turn to play." It uses "just five main categories: outplay, exchange, comparison, layout and other," and notes that because games absorb ideas from one another, the real structure "is not a tree but a rather complex network."[^1]
2. **Objective is a second, independent axis.** Pagat's objective classes are capturing cards; shedding or accumulating; forming combinations; comparing; and other.[^1] Crossing mechanism with objective explains most of the apparent disagreements between schemes.
3. **Collectible, living and deck-building games differ structurally from traditional games in the deck, not the play.** In traditional games every player draws from one shared, symmetric deck. In CCGs, LCGs and deck-builders each player has a personal deck, built before play (CCG or LCG) or during play (deck-builders), and cards carry printed rules text instead of just rank and suit.[^33] [^34] [^41]
4. **Pagat reports that Crazy Eights is played under very many names:** "Crates, Switch, Swedish Rummy, Last One or Rockaway. In Germany it is Mau-Mau; in Switzerland it is Tschausepp; in the Netherlands it is Pesten". Pagat also notes that "it would be rare to find two groups that play exactly the same way."[^2]
5. **Shithead has the most disputed special-card table of any folk game.** Pagat's baseline has 2s resetting the pile, 10s burning it, and four-of-a-kind also burning. Variations documented by named pagat contributors (Jim Curtis, Ciaran Gultnieks, Sean Daly, William Putt, Jane Guarducci and Cristian Seres) give 7s, 8s, jokers and even 5s different powers. Pagat's own advice on one joker rule is "To avoid arguments decide before starting which of these variations is being played."[^10]
6. **Uno's most argued rule, stacking Draw cards, is officially not allowed.** On May 4, 2019 the official @realUNOgame account posted: "If someone puts down a +4 card, you must draw 4 and your turn is skipped. You can't put down a +2 to make the next person draw 6. We know you've tried it." Zhané Lloyd's "We Play by House Rules" (American Journal of Play, 2021) records that the post drew "over eighty thousand likes, ninety thousand retweets, and nearly six thousand replies." Stacking nonetheless survives as a house rule ("Progressive Uno"); UNO itself explained that its mobile version "features custom rules, including the most common house rules."[^48] [^49]
7. **Speed games settle simultaneous claims in one of three ways.** Either the physically lowest or first hand on the pile wins; or the cards go to a shared "pool" to be won later (Snap); or the slowest player loses (Pig, Spoons, Irish Snap).[^61] [^65] [^70] Pre-agreeing which method applies is the single most useful house rule.
8. **Nearly all traditional game names are generic.** Proprietary names include UNO and Skip-Bo (Mattel), Exploding Kittens, Top Trumps and Whot (Winning Moves UK), Dominion (Rio Grande), Magic: The Gathering (Wizards of the Coast), Hearthstone (Blizzard), and "Living Card Game"/"LCG" (Fantasy Flight).[^76] [^77] [^78] [^80] [^81] [^82] [^84] [^85] [^86] [^87]

## Classification

### Recommended family scheme

The scheme below keeps pagat's mechanism-first logic and uses the family names players actually recognise. Proprietary families are added at the end.

| 1 | Plain-trick (outplay) | Each player plays one card; highest of the led suit or highest trump wins the trick; count tricks | Win (or bid exactly) a number of tricks | Whist, Knockout Whist, Spades, Bridge, Oh Hell, Euchre |
| 2 | Point-trick (outplay) | As above, but card values in captured tricks score | Capture card points | Skat, Belote, Jass, Pinochle, Sheepshead |

### Where classifications disagree

- **Mechanism versus objective.** Parlett and pagat classify by mechanism. Wikipedia and many rulebooks group by objective ("shedding games", "trick-avoidance games"). So pagat puts Cheat with Shithead under *beating*, while Wikipedia's shedding category places Cheat beside Crazy Eights. Both are defensible. Cheat's bluff-and-challenge loop is its real distinguishing mechanic, which is why many modern lists treat bluffing as its own family.
- **War.** Pagat keeps War in the "war group" with Beggar-my-Neighbour. ETG Design, which draws its broad divisions from Parlett and its detailed groups from pagat, argues that "the simultaneity of play in War means it is essentially a Comparison game." It keeps Beggar-my-Neighbour-style "forced-play games" separate.[^7]
- **Spit/Speed.** Pagat files it under competitive patience because the core action is building up or down on shared foundation piles. Popular sources call it a "shedding" or "speed" game.[^60] The mechanism is patience; the experience is real-time racing.
- **Shithead.** Pagat treats it as a beating game (you must beat or pick up). Some sources lump it with climbing games like President. The pick-up penalty is the decisive difference: climbing games let you pass for free.[^10]
- **Eights versus Stops.** Pagat's "matching games" umbrella holds both the Eights group (Crazy Eights, Uno) and the older Stops group (Newmarket, Snip Snap Snorum). Wikipedia describes Mao as "from a subset of the Stops family."[^11]
- **Proprietary games.** Pagat mostly keeps these in a "commercial" or "other" bucket. BoardGameGeek tags each game with multiple mechanics rather than one family, so its tags work better as a list of primitives than as a family tree.[^35]

## Families

Player counts and game lengths below are typical figures, not hard limits. Timing means turn-based, simultaneous, or with out-of-turn plays allowed.

### 1–3. Trick-taking (plain, point, avoidance)

**Defining mechanic:** each player in turn contributes one card to a *trick*. Players must usually follow the suit led. The trick goes to the highest card of the led suit, or to the highest trump. Plain-trick games count tricks, point-trick games count the card values captured, and avoidance games penalise certain captures. Pagat defines a trick as "each player in turn playing one card face up to the table."[^1]

- **Whist:** 4 players in fixed partnerships with a 52-card deck, 13 cards each. Trump is traditionally set by turning up the dealer's last card. A side scores one point per trick above six (the first six tricks are the "book"). It is the ancestor of Bridge and Spades.
- **Knockout Whist:** 2–7 players. The hand size falls by one card each deal, and a player who takes no tricks is knocked out. Wikipedia calls it "a good way to teach the concept of tricks to children."[^13]
- **Spades:** 4 players in partnerships; "spades are always trumps" (pagat). Each player bids a number of tricks from 0 to 13, and partners add their bids together. A made contract scores 10 per trick bid plus 1 per overtrick. Overtricks are "bags": "A side which (over several deals) accumulates ten or more bags has 100 points deducted from its score." A nil bid is worth ±100 and blind nil ±200. The game is played to 500.[^14] [^63]
- **Contract Bridge:** 4 players in partnerships with a 52-card deck. There is an auction over 7 levels and 5 denominations, from "one club" up to "seven no trumps". The declarer's partner is the **dummy**, whose hand is exposed after the opening lead and who takes no active part in the play. Home games use rubber scoring (best of three games, each to 100 points); clubs and tournaments play duplicate.[^15]
- **Hearts:** 3–6 players, best with 4. Each heart costs 1 point and the Q♠ costs 13. There is a pre-play card pass. Taking every penalty card is "shooting the moon," which reverses the penalty. The game ends when someone reaches 100 and the lowest score wins.[^16] [^17]

**Players/length:** 3–6, most often 4; about 5–15 minutes per hand, and a game of 30–90 minutes. **Hidden information:** each hand is hidden from everyone else. Bidding and play leak information, which partners exploit legally through conventions. The widow or kitty is hidden until taken. Bridge's dummy is fully public. **Timing:** strictly turn-based.

### 4. Climbing

**Defining mechanic:** beat the previous play with a higher single, pair, run or combination, or pass. When everyone passes, the last player to play leads fresh. Pagat: "if you cannot or do not wish to beat the previous play you simply pass."[^1]

- **President (also called Asshole or Scum):** 4–8 players. The finishing order sets ranks for the next deal, and the lowest-ranked players must hand their best cards to the top-ranked. Pagat lists it among games "children and young people sometimes prefer to play".
- **Big Two / Zheng Shangyou:** 4 players. Players shed singles, pairs, triples and poker-style five-card hands; 2s rank highest.

**Players/length:** 4–8; 5–10 minutes per hand. **Hidden:** hands. **Timing:** turn-based; some variants allow out-of-turn "bombs".

### 5. Beating (beat or pick up)

**Defining mechanic:** "At your turn you must either beat the card played by the previous player or pick it up (possibly with other cards) and add it to your hand" (pagat).[^10]

- **Shithead (Palace, Karma, China Hand, Ten-Two Slide):** pagat says "From two to five may play. The game is best is with at least three"; 6 can play with jokers. Each player has 3 face-down cards, 3 face-up cards on top of them, and a 3-card hand. You play equal or higher cards to the pile, or pick the pile up. 2s can be played on anything and anything can be played on them. A 10 or a four-of-a-kind burns the pile, and the same player goes again. The last player holding cards is the "shithead." Pagat suggests it is "probably of Scandinavian or Nordic origin", related to Swedish Vändtia and Finnish Paskahousu, and that it has spread "during the last decades of the 20th century by young travellers (backpackers) of all nationalities."[^10]
- **Durak:** a Russian game for 2–6 players, played as attacker against defender. The defender must beat each attacking card or pick up all the cards played; the last player holding cards loses.
- **Cheat / BS / I Doubt It:** see Variants. Cards are played face down with a claimed rank. A caught liar, or a wrong challenger, picks up the whole pile.[^6] [^57]

**Players/length:** 3–6 (Cheat up to 10); 10–20 minutes. **Hidden:** hands. In Shithead, face-down table cards are hidden even from their owner. **Timing:** turn-based; some house rules allow out-of-turn completion of a four-of-a-kind.

### 6. Matching and shedding (Eights and Stops)

**Defining mechanic:** play a card that matches the previous card's rank or suit, or that fits a sequence; otherwise draw. Special ranks skip, reverse, force draws or change suit.

- **Crazy Eights:** 2–8 players, with 5 cards each (7 each for two players). Match suit or rank; an 8 is wild and the player names a suit. Pagat's typical extras are Queen skips, Ace reverses and Two forces the next player to draw 2 (stackable). The first out wins and the others score penalties: 50 per eight, 10 per picture card, pips at face value.[^2]
- **Switch (UK):** the same engine, but "in the British game Switch it is often the Ace" that changes suit (pagat). Some British players call it "Black Jack", a name that collides with the casino game.[^2]
- **Mau-Mau (Germany):** played with 32 cards. Typically Jacks are wild, and you must call "Mau" on your last card; exact special cards vary by region.[^18] [^47]
- **Uno:** the proprietary version of the Eights engine, with a coloured deck and printed Skip, Reverse, Draw Two, Wild and Wild Draw Four cards. In a two-player game, Reverse acts as Skip.[^19]

**Players/length:** 2–8; 5–10 minutes per hand. **Hidden:** hands. **Timing:** turn-based, but with out-of-turn *penalty calls*, such as failing to announce "last card".

### 7. Draw-and-discard (Rummy)

**Defining mechanic:** "The basic move is to draw a card from the stock and discard one to the discard pile" (pagat). Players form *melds*: sets of equal rank, and runs in suit.[^55]

- **Rummy (basic):** 2–6 players. Draw, meld sets or runs, lay cards off on existing melds, and discard; the first player out wins. Penalty points are counted from the cards left in hand.
- **Gin Rummy:** 2 players, 10 cards each. Nobody melds until the hand ends. You may **knock** with 10 or fewer points of unmatched cards ("deadwood"); a hand with no deadwood is **gin**. The opponent may lay off cards on the knocker's melds. If the opponent's deadwood is equal or lower, the knocker is **undercut** and the opponent scores the bonus. The game is usually played to 100.[^20] [^55]
- **Canasta:** 4 players in partnerships with two decks plus jokers. 2s and jokers are wild. Players build 7-card "canastas", and the discard pile can be frozen.

**Players/length:** 2–6; Gin hands take about 5 minutes and Canasta games an hour. **Hidden:** hands and the stock. The discard pile is public, and in some games is only partly visible. **Timing:** turn-based. Some variants let other players claim a discard out of turn (a "buy" in Kalooki-style games).

### 8. Asking, commerce and passing

**Defining mechanic:** you gain cards from other players or from a pool: by asking (quartet), exchanging with a face-up pool (commerce), or passing cards around the circle (passing).

- **Go Fish / Happy Families:** 2–6 players. Ask a named player for a rank you hold. If they have none, "go fish" from the stock. Four of a kind makes a book.[^22] [^62]
- **Pig / Spoons:** 3–13 players, using four cards of one rank per player. Everyone passes one card to the left **simultaneously**. A player who completes four of a kind touches their nose (Pig) or grabs a spoon (Spoons), and the slowest player loses.[^5] [^24] [^70] [^90]
- **31 / Schwimmen / Scat:** players swap cards with a face-up pool to reach the highest same-suit total; anyone may knock to end the round.

**Players/length:** 3–10; 2–10 minutes. **Hidden:** hands. In Go Fish, earlier questions publicly reveal part of each hand, so memory matters. **Timing:** Go Fish is turn-based; Pig and Spoons are simultaneous.

### 9. Fishing or capture

**Defining mechanic:** "There is a pool of face-up cards on the table which can be captured by playing a matching card from your hand" (pagat).[^1]

- **Scopa (Italy):** played with a 40-card Italian deck. Capture by matching a card's rank or a sum of table cards. Clearing the table is a *scopa* and scores a point.
- **Casino / Cassino:** players can also "build" combinations on the table and capture them on a later turn.

**Players/length:** 2–4; 15–30 minutes. **Hidden:** hands; the table is public. **Timing:** turn-based.

### 10. Adding

**Defining mechanic:** "The cards are played in a pile and their values added, the aim usually being to achieve or avoid particular totals" (pagat).[^1]

- **Cribbage:** 2–4 players. In the "pegging" play, cards are played to a running count that cannot pass 31, and 15s, pairs and runs score. Hands and the dealer's crib are then scored. Progress is recorded on a peg board, and the game is to 121.
- **Ninety-Nine (adding version):** push the running total towards 99; a player who is forced over 99 loses a life. (Not to be confused with Parlett's trick-taking game of the same name.)[^25]

**Players/length:** 2–6; 15–30 minutes. **Hidden:** hands; the crib stays hidden until scored. **Timing:** turn-based.

### 11. Vying and showdown (Poker family)

**Defining mechanic:** before hands are compared, "players may propose to raise the stakes, and those who do not wish to accept the raise may drop out" (pagat). Parlett distinguishes two vying methods: "the Equalization method (Poker style) and the Matching method (Brag style)."[^26] [^28]

- **Texas Hold'em:** 2–10 players. Each player gets two private hole cards, and blinds are posted. Betting rounds come before the flop (three shared cards), after the flop, after the turn and after the river. The best 5-card hand made from any of the 7 available cards wins.[^27]
- **Omaha:** each player gets four hole cards, and must use "exactly two hole cards plus exactly three of the five cards on the table" (pagat).[^56]
- **Five-card draw:** each player gets five private cards, bets, draws once to replace cards, bets again, and goes to showdown. It is often played "Jacks or Better" to open.[^29]

**Players/length:** 2–10; one to three minutes per hand, and sessions are open-ended. **Hidden:** hole cards. Community cards are public; in draw poker, how many cards someone draws is public. **Timing:** turn-based betting.

### 12. Banking

**Defining mechanic:** "The players do not play against each other, but each plays individually against a special player - the banker" (pagat).[^30]

- **Blackjack (casino 21):** a permanent dealer follows fixed hit/stand rules. A two-card 21 (a "blackjack") pays a bonus. Options include hit, stand, double, split and sometimes surrender or insurance.
- **Pontoon (UK home 21):** a rotating banker. Players choose from Twist, Buy, Stick and Split. Special hands are the Pontoon (a two-card 21) and the Five Card Trick. The banker wins ties, and a player who makes a Pontoon takes over the bank (pagat).[^30]
- **Vingt-et-un:** the French ancestor of both. Pagat notes that "The game Pontoon and its name are derived from the French Vingt-et-un (21)."[^30]

**Players/length:** 2–8+; Pontoon "works well with 5 to 8 players" (pagat).[^30] Hands take about a minute. **Hidden:** the banker's hole card or cards, and players' face-down cards. **Timing:** turn-based around the table, with the banker playing last.

### 13. War and slap games

**Defining mechanic:** "Players store their cards in a pile face down and turn up the top card to play it. In certain circumstances the played cards are captured by one player" (pagat).[^1]

- **War:** two players reveal cards simultaneously and the higher card wins. A tie triggers a "war" in which each player stakes extra face-down cards.
- **Beggar-my-Neighbour:** a face card demands 1–4 cards from the next player (Jack 1, Queen 2, King 3, Ace 4). If none of those is a face card, the player of the face card takes the pile.
- **Snap:** players turn cards in turn onto their own piles; the first to call "Snap!" on matching ranks wins the piles.[^31] [^64]
- **Slapjack:** the first player to slap a played Jack wins the central pile.[^3] [^4]
- **Egyptian Ratscrew:** Beggar-my-Neighbour plus slapping patterns such as doubles and sandwiches.[^32] [^66]

**Players/length:** 2–8; 5–30 minutes, and games can be long. **Hidden:** personal piles are hidden from *everyone*, owner included. **Timing:** turn-based card flipping, with **out-of-turn, real-time claims**.

### 14. Patience and competitive patience

**Defining mechanic:** build cards onto foundations or a tableau according to sequence rules. The competitive forms race on shared foundations.

- **Klondike (Solitaire):** 1 player; a 7-column tableau, building suit foundations from Ace up.
- **Spit/Speed:** 2 players building up or down on two shared spit piles simultaneously; details in Variants.[^8] [^9]
- **Nerts / Racing Demon:** 2–6 players each run a personal patience while sharing Ace foundations.

**Players/length:** 1–6; 2–10 minutes. **Hidden:** face-down tableau cards; in Speed, the hand. **Timing:** simultaneous and real-time in the competitive forms.

### 15. Proprietary families: CCG, LCG, deck-building, drafting, elimination

These use custom decks, but their mechanics map onto the same primitives.

- **Collectible card games (Magic: The Gathering; Hearthstone, which is digital):** each player brings a *constructed* personal deck from a collection acquired in randomised packs. Cards carry rules text; there is a resource system (lands or mana) and a combat zone of permanents on a tableau. Wikipedia defines a CCG as "a game where players acquire cards into a personal collection from which they create customized decks of cards and challenge other players in matches."[^33]
- **Living/expandable card games:** the same constructed-deck play, but with fixed, non-random distribution. Fantasy Flight describes LCGs as keeping CCG-style expansion while doing "away with the deterrent of the blind-buy purchase model".[^34] [^36]
- **Deck-builders (Dominion):** everyone starts with an identical small deck; Dominion's is 7 Coppers and 3 Estates. Players buy cards from a common supply during play, and the draw–play–discard–reshuffle cycle is the core engine. Vaccarino explains the design: "you draw 5 cards a turn. You recycle everything so you can keep drawing it." Wikipedia calls Dominion (2008) "the first deck-building game."[^37] [^38] [^39] [^40] [^41]
- **Drafting (Sushi Go):** players "choose one card at the same time, reveal it, then pass what is left to the player on your left", over three rounds. It combines simultaneous selection with set-collection scoring.[^12] [^42] [^43] [^88] [^89]
- **Press-your-luck elimination (Exploding Kittens):** you must draw a card to end your turn, and drawing an Exploding Kitten eliminates you unless you play a Defuse card. The deck holds one Kitten fewer than the number of players. Nope cards can be played "at any time, even if it's not your turn."[^44] [^45]

**Structural differences from traditional games:** decks are asymmetric and owned by individual players; rules live on the cards rather than in rank and suit; players manage persistent resources and board states; and deck construction is a metagame outside the match, which deck-builders move inside the game.

## Primitives

### Catalogue of recurring building blocks

- **Decks:** full 52; 52 plus jokers; stripped 40 (Italian/Spanish), 36 (Jass/German), 32 (Skat/Mau-Mau), 24 (Euchre); multiple decks (Canasta, big Pontoon or Crazy Eights groups); fixed wilds (Crazy Eights 8s, Canasta 2s and jokers) or chosen wilds (poker "deuces wild"); tarot trumps; proprietary decks.
- **Deals:** whole deck (Whist, Hearts, War, Cheat); hand plus stock (Rummy, Crazy Eights, Go Fish); batches (Skat 3-4-3); layered face-down/face-up/hand (Shithead); progressive (Knockout Whist, Pontoon, Stud); rotating dealer versus fixed banker.
- **Zones:** hand; stock; discard; tableau; current trick; captured tricks; personal face-down and face-up table cards (Shithead, Spit); kitty, widow or skat; community cards (Hold'em); crib; personal decks and discards (deck-builders).
- **Ranks and trumps:** Ace high or low; around-the-corner wrapping (Spit); ace–ten orders (Belote, Jass); unusual top cards (Shithead 2s, Big Two 2s, Put 3s); trump by turn-up (Whist), bid (Bridge), fixed (Spades) or none (Hearts).
- **Play structures:** trick; meld; match; beat or pass; beat or pick up; capture; running total; comparison; bidding (contract or prediction); betting and vying; buying; drafting; asking; passing.
- **Information states:** private hand; public table; hidden from everyone including the owner (Shithead face-down cards, War piles); partially revealed (Go Fish questions); declared but unverified (Cheat's claims).
- **Timing:** strict turn order; simultaneous action (Sushi Go, Pig, Spit); out-of-turn interrupts (Snap, Slapjack, Nope, "last card" calls, Cheat challenges).
- **Scoring and end:** first out; last out loses (Shithead); trick or card points; bid made or failed; penalty threshold (Hearts to 100); race to target (Cribbage 121, Spades 500); elimination with lives (Pig letters); win all cards (War group); chips (banking, vying).

## Speed and reaction games: how ties and simultaneous claims are resolved

| Game | Real-time element | Tie / simultaneous-claim rule | False-claim penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snap | Call "Snap!" on matching ranks | Matching piles go to a **snap pool**, won later by calling "Snap pool!"; or grab a central object, and the hand underneath wins (pagat and derivatives) | Your face-up pile goes to the pool, or you give a card |
| Slapjack | Slap any Jack | "the one whose hand is directly on top of the jack wins the pile" (Bicycle), meaning the hand touching the card | Give a card to the player of the card |
| Egyptian Ratscrew | Slap doubles, sandwiches, top-bottom, marriages, tens, jokers, etc. | Hand touching the pile wins; Bicycle also offers "the person with the most fingers on top wins" | Burn 1–2 cards into the pile. Out-of-cards players may "slap in" |
| Spit/Speed | Simultaneous builds | "A card counts as played as soon as it touches the pile" (pagat); when both play to the same pile, the first card down stays; a contested pile choice goes to the lower hand | None standard |
| Spoons | Grab a spoon (one fewer than players) | Once the first spoon is taken, anyone may grab; the player left without one gets a letter (S-P-O-O-N-S, or P-I-G) | Some groups penalise grabbing a spoon without four of a kind |
| Pig | Touch your nose | Last to notice loses | — |
| Irish Snap | Slap on a called-rank match | **Slowest** slapper takes the pile | — |
| Cheat (challenges) | Call "Cheat" before the next play | First to touch the stack and call, or nearest in rotation to the dealer's left | Wrong challenger picks up the pile |
| Exploding Kittens (Nope) | Out-of-turn Nope | Nope chains resolve in order ("Nope a Nope" makes a "Yup") | — |

Sources: pagat's Snap, Slapjack, Spit and Spoons pages;[^3] [^8] [^64] [^90] Bicycle's Slapjack and Egyptian Rat Screw rules;[^4] [^66] Cardanoir's and Gamerules.com's Egyptian Ratscrew write-ups;[^67] [^68] CardRules+ and Family Game Shelf on Spoons;[^70] [^71] PlayingCardDecks.com on Pig and Spoons;[^72] 64 Ounce Games' and Asmodee's Exploding Kittens rules for the Nope chain.[^44] [^45]

**Design lesson:** real-time games need three explicit rules: what triggers a valid claim, how physical ties are broken (usually lowest hand on the pile), and what a false claim costs. Without all three, arguments dominate the game.

## Shortlist: games for casual groups of 2–8 with newcomers

Games are ranked on four criteria: time to learn, round length, interaction, and ease of joining between rounds.

1. **Cheat / BS (3–10).** The rules fit in one sentence, and everyone acts or reacts every turn. Rounds take 10–15 minutes, and a newcomer can join at the next deal. Bluffing rewards social skills, which levels the field for novices.
2. **Crazy Eights / Switch / Mau-Mau (2–8).** Almost everyone knows a version, and hands take 5 minutes. Joining is trivial because the deal is fresh each hand. Its weakness is the house-rule clashes, so agree the special-card table first.
3. **Spoons / Pig (3–13).** The simultaneous play means no waiting, and it works for the largest groups. It needs only a subset of a deck and some spoons. Each round lasts under two minutes.
4. **Shithead / Palace (3–5, 6 with jokers).** Pagat gives it top ratings for popularity and trend (5 of 5 each) and a difficulty of only 2, and it has a satisfying comeback arc through the face-down cards.[^10] It is slightly harder to teach because of the special cards.
5. **Pontoon / home 21 (3–8).** Pagat says it "works well with 5 to 8 players."[^30] Hands take about a minute, anyone can join at any deal, and the only decision is twist or stick. Play for matchsticks.
6. **Go Fish / Happy Families (2–6).** The best choice for mixed ages and children, with no reading or maths needed. Interaction is low for adults.
7. **President (4–8).** A climbing game with a social-hierarchy twist that makes repeated rounds fun. Learning the combination rules takes one round.
8. **Knockout Whist (2–7).** The gentlest introduction to trick-taking, but knocked-out players wait.[^13]
9. **Snap / Slapjack / Egyptian Ratscrew (2–6).** Best for children and energetic groups; ratscrew games can drag on.
10. **Proprietary picks:** Uno (2–10) is familiar to everyone; Sushi Go (2–5) is simultaneous; Exploding Kittens (2–5) plays fast.

**Avoid for mixed-experience casual groups:** Bridge (fixed partnerships, long learning); Hearts (best only with exactly 4); Gin Rummy and Spit (2 players only, though Spit is an excellent side game).

## Caveats

- Folk-game rules have no authority: as the Wikipedia article on pagat notes, the site "does not provide official rules to 'folk games' as none generally exist, but rather reports how the games are actually played."[^92] The "baseline" rules above are pagat's reference descriptions, not canonical rules.
- Several variant sources (Official Game Rules, Cardanoir, CardRules+, Joker Palace) are secondary or commercial rule sites, used only to confirm common house rules. The out-of-turn four-of-a-kind burn in Shithead rests on anecdotal reports.[^46]
- Trademark data comes mostly from Justia and Trademarkia copies of USPTO records; verify current status in USPTO's TSDR or EUIPO before relying on it legally. No registration was found for Sushi Go, and the absence of registrations for traditional names is inferred, not proven.
